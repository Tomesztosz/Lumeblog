1969 is the year of the automatic chronograph. Three camps got there at roughly the same
moment, and Zenith arrived with the one thing that would still set its movement apart
sixty years later: frequency.

## Why 36,000 matters

El Primero runs at 36,000 vibrations per hour — 5 hertz. Most of the field at the time
sat at 21,600 (3 Hz). That has one very tangible consequence: the chronograph seconds
hand doesn't take nine or ten steps per second, it takes ten, and the chronograph can
resolve a tenth of a second straight off the dial. This isn't theoretical precision, it's
*legible* precision — exactly the distinction this journal cares about.

There was a price. A faster beat consumes more energy, loads the escapement harder, and
is fussier about lubrication. High frequency is an engineering decision, not a marketing
number.

## Then came quartz

In the mid-seventies Zenith's American owner decided that mechanical movements had no
future. Production was to stop and the tooling — the presses, jigs, cutters, plans — was
to be scrapped.

Charles Vermot, a watchmaker at the Le Locle factory, didn't scrap it. He carried it up
into the attic, documented it, and walled it in.

The story reads well on its own, but the romance isn't the point. A mechanical calibre
doesn't exist because you still have the drawings. It exists because you still have the
tooling that lets a particular set of machines produce it to a particular tolerance. Lose
that, and the calibre isn't restarted — it's redesigned, at years and an order of
magnitude more money.

## Why it came back

In the eighties the movement was wanted again, and because of Vermot's material it didn't
have to start from zero. One sentence holds the whole thing: one man's professional
judgement, against the corporate decision, preserved a calibre that is still running.

Not heroism. More the recognition that the knowledge doesn't live in the paperwork. It
lives in the tooling.
