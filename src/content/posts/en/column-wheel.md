The hard part of a chronograph isn't measuring time. The measuring is already there,
running underneath. The hard part is *coordination*: when the pusher goes down, the
counting train has to start, the clutch has to engage and the brake has to release — three
things, from one movement, in the right order.

There are two established solutions to that coordination.

## The column wheel

A column wheel is a small toothed wheel with vertical columns standing on top of it, like
a tiny crown. Each press of the pusher advances it by one tooth. The levers that control
the individual chronograph functions rest on that crown: they either *sit up* on a column
or *drop* into the gap between two.

That's the whole idea. A single angular position of one wheel tells all three levers what
to do. Control lives in one place, and the sequence is guaranteed geometrically — it
doesn't depend on timing.

## The cam

The other solution is a shaped cam (cam or coulisse lever). It does the same work, but
distributes the motion through profiled surfaces. It is cheaper to make, simpler to
adjust, and at least as reliable mechanically.

Where it differs: along the cam's profile the resistance at the pusher changes, and it
usually ends in a more definite click. With a column wheel the lever's drop is short and
clean — that is the "silky" pusher feel people attribute to column wheels. They aren't
imagining it. It really does come from there.

## What it doesn't do

A column wheel does **not** make the watch more accurate. Rate accuracy lives in the
escapement and the hairspring, not in the chronograph's control layer. Nor does it do
more: it switches exactly the same functions. What it gives you is the quality of the
switching and — let's be honest — the view through the caseback.

The real precision question is decided elsewhere: whether the counting wheel is driven
through a *lateral* or a *vertical* clutch. That decision is what determines whether the
seconds hand jumps when you start it. But that's the next piece.
