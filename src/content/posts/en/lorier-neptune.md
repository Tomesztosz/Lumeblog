There is a quiet contradiction in the way we wear dive watches. The device on the wrist
was built for a concrete engineering job: stay legible in the dark, keep water out, and
let a rotating bezel measure how much air is left. Then, for most of them, the deepest
dive of their lives is the washing-up.

That doesn't make it a lie. It's more about what a person keeps of an object once the
function is no longer needed.

## What the hand notices first

Lorier is a small workshop in New York, run by a married couple. The Neptune is the piece
that takes its cue from sixties dive watches — not as a style quotation but in the
proportions: a relatively small case, a domed plastic crystal, and a dial with nothing
surplus on it.

The domed acrylic gives itself away immediately in the hand. It scratches — that's a
fact, and the owner will know exactly where within two months. In exchange it gives the
edge of the dial a warm, slightly distorted look that sapphire cannot imitate. The
scratch polishes out with a bit of plastic polish; with sapphire it doesn't.

## The bezel as the most honest control

The only real control on a dive watch is the rotating bezel. It is unidirectional for a
reason: if it gets nudged, it can only move in the direction that makes the remaining
time look *shorter*. The direction of the failure is designed in.

That is the kind of decision that makes an object trustworthy: not that it never fails,
but that it knows which way to fail.

## Why the small workshop is worth it

A microbrand isn't interesting because it's cheaper. It's interesting because at this
size there is a person behind every decision who can tell you why it is the way it is.
Why this crystal. Why the hands are this wide. Why there is no date window. At the big
houses those answers get lost in the process.

Taken in hand, the Neptune isn't about depth. It's about someone following a thought all
the way through.
